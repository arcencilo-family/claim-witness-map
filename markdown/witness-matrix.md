## Notes & commentary

Credibility · confidence · the two markers · fair witnessing · using both matrices

### Y axis - Credibility

G stands for credibility, from the German Glaubwürdigkeit. Assess the source for this task from a relevant record and the reasons available. Note how that record was checked: being thoroughly tested is not itself a favourable result.

G-high: a relevant reliable record. G-mixed: relevant strengths and failures. G-unknown: too little basis to judge. G-low: relevant reliability failures. CONDITIONAL labels mean weighing the relevant strengths and failures.

Unknown is not low. Familiarity, status, and confident delivery do not establish reliability. A source assessment is revisable; its supporting claims can themselves be checked.

### X axis - Stated confidence

The columns describe the certainty the source expresses about this claim. Keep their actual words or number; the example phrases are guides, not a numerical conversion scale.

If confidence is unstated, leave the column unassigned rather than inventing it.

Confidence is not credibility. Greater certainty earns no automatic pass; tentative wording proves neither honesty nor poor reliability. A reliable record does not establish that each confidence statement is calibrated.

### The positive marker - Best value

G-high / Certain combines a favourable source assessment with an explicit, certain account. The positive marker highlights this favourable source profile: a credible source is speaking with certainty.

Give that account weight alongside the evidence. The marker does not mean certainty makes the source more credible than an equally reliable but cautious witness, or prove that this claim is true.

The green row acknowledges the same favourable relevant record across all four confidence columns.

### The warning marker - Danger

G-low / Certain combines relevant reliability problems with emphatic certainty. The danger is that decisive delivery may persuade the reader to overlook those problems.

Seek evidence independent of the relevant failure. The marker does not declare dishonesty or automatically reject this particular account; a source with an adverse record can still be right.

An unknown but certain source belongs in G-unknown, not in this warning cell merely for being unfamiliar.

### From oath to fair witnessing

An honesty pledge asks for truthful reporting. Fair witnessing adds a practical discipline: separate what was observed, inferred, and left unknown.

> I will tell what I know from where I stand.  
> I will say how sure I am.  
> I will not fabricate.  
> I will correct what I learn to be wrong.  

A witness can be certain about the visible side of a house without claiming to know its other sides. Precision about scope need not mean uncertainty.

The pledge expresses commitment; the matrices support examination. It does not replace a prescribed legal oath or affirmation.

### Use both matrices together

Locate the source here, then assess the claim in the Claim Matrix. Ask which part still depends on this account and what independent support remains.

Readers may disagree about placement. The useful next step is to identify the disputed reason or missing check, not to treat the coloured cell as a verdict.

This is a proposed discussion aid. It has not been validated as a jury instrument; applicable evidentiary rules, burdens of proof, and judicial directions govern any court use.
